# Near-term disposal must preserve high-apogee staging and survive PuffSat failure

Status: accepted. Atmospheric baseline and failure treatment agreed in the
2026-09-10 grill and incorporated into the paper. The existing remote-ocean
targeting remains; route-specific operating constraints are not determined here.

The user requires passive disposal after complete loss of PuffSat function. Retain
high-apogee deployment as the working requirement because its slow motion permits
low-cost adjustments to interception timing and spacing. Compare atmospheric
disposal and Earth escape as the two leading near-term strategies. Lunar impact
and other routes are outside this recommendation.

Atmospheric disposal is the near-term baseline. An intact PuffSat on the
existing low-perigee staging ellipse returns to the atmosphere without a later
disposal command. A forward escape burn at
apogee instead makes that distant point the new perigee and loses the low-altitude
interception. For a 150,000 km apogee and an original 200 km perigee, making an
inbound hyperbola that reaches 200 km at local escape speed plus 25 m/s requires
about 2.33 km/s at the staging point in a two-body calculation. It is not a small
adjustment to the bound staging orbit.

The existing 50 km disposal perigee remains a working value, not an optimized
altitude or a guarantee of complete hardware demise. Raising it to 100 km lowers
the PuffSat's downward velocity at 200 km by about 18%. The corresponding incoming
angle seen by a rocket moving horizontally at 7.8 km/s falls from 29.1 to 24.1
degrees. Neither angle is a required plate tilt; the plate's transverse momentum
transfer is not specified by the paper's scalar coupling factor.

Energetic breakup is accepted as a separate residual debris risk. The user expects
it to be uncommon; no event rate has been estimated. The baseline does not require
a staging or hardware redesign solely to guarantee passive disposal of every
breakup fragment. Breakup changes individual fragment trajectories, so the intact
PuffSat's disposal orbit cannot provide that guarantee.

Atmospheric entry does not establish complete vaporization of the dry hardware,
so ground-fragment risk is separate from removing an intact missed PuffSat from
Earth orbit. Retain the paper's remote-ocean targeting without claiming that all
components vaporize or that a detailed footprint has been established.

At the 150,000 km apogee, a forward tangential fragment impulse of about 1.67 m/s
raises a 50 km perigee to 100 km; about 5.00 m/s raises it to 200 km. These are
two-body orbit changes, not debris-lifetime estimates. The cheap targeting
adjustments that motivate high-apogee staging also make breakup there sensitive.

The user's 100 billion objects per century is a traffic stress case, not an
established forecast. At that rate, 250 g discarded per object gives 250,000
tonnes/year. The user proposes evaluating a composition with only 50 g of silicon
and metal per object, giving 50,000 tonnes/year of those materials. That is a
composition scenario rather than the current hardware budget: the paper's
aluminum propellant tank alone masses 88 g. Comparison with natural influx must
distinguish atmospheric entry from deposition at the surface and account for
material composition.

[Schulz and Glassmeier (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13032),
doi:10.1016/j.asr.2020.10.036, estimate natural atmospheric injection of about
12,325 tonnes/year, including 4,047 tonnes of metals and 1,655 tonnes of
metalloids. Their inventory excludes large impacts occurring less often than
once per decade. The user's 50,000 tonnes/year silicon-and-metal scenario is
about four times that natural total, or nine times its metals-plus-metalloids
component. This supports a broad order-of-magnitude comparison with natural
influx, not equal elemental loading or an established environmental effect.
