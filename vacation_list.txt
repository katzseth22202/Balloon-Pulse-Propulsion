## Idea 1: vacuum-bake the TPU liner and qualify it to ASTM E595

### Where it goes

Line 307, the frozen-ISRU inner-liner paragraph in the two-shell section. That paragraph
already picks thermoplastic polyurethane for the compliant liner, on the grounds that it
is the bladder material of inflatable spacecraft and pressure suits, and that polyether
grades resist hydrolysis where polyester grades would not. It says nothing about
outgassing.

### The claim to add

TPU is the one polymer in the two-shell stack with a real outgassing problem. It carries
residual volatiles, and polyether grades in particular are not automatically low-outgassing.
The fix is the standard one: qualify to ASTM E595 (TML ≤ 1.0%, CVCM ≤ 0.10%, the NASA
screening thresholds) and vacuum-bake the liner before flight so it meets them.

This matters more here than on a typical spacecraft, because the liner sits *inside* the
water volume and the outer bumper is the thermal control surface. Condensable volatiles
have somewhere to go and something to fog.

### Why this is easy to write

The paper already makes exactly this move once, at line 1006, for the Ceres acrylic
concentrating optic. It vacuum-bakes the optic before flight and cites two existing
bib entries:

- `outgassing_rp1124` — NASA RP-1124, the outgassing data compilation
- `outgassing_temperature_decay` — Arrhenius temperature dependence of outgassing rate

Both are reusable verbatim. The Arrhenius argument transfers well: line 1006 notes the
screening test runs at 125 °C, and a frozen-ISRU PuffSat runs far colder, so a bake-dried
liner sheds volatiles far more slowly than its screening number suggests.

`ASTM E595` does not currently appear anywhere in the paper (`grep -i e595` returns
nothing). It will need its own bib entry. Line 232 mentions outgassing of the explosives,
and line 303 says the PEN inner skin holds water vapor "without outgassing", so there is
already vocabulary to hook into.

### Open question

Whether to also assert an E595-qualified TPU grade exists off the shelf, or state it as a
screening requirement on the liner. The second is safer and matches how the paper handles
the coated-PBO barrier at line 1506. Prefer that unless a specific qualified grade turns up.

## Idea 2: phase-change ballast under the PuffSat cameras

### The idea, in Seth's framing

> I want to investigate the small cameras on the PuffSat dry mass. I'm wondering if we can
> lay them on some additional mass like a phase change material to ensure they don't suffer
> thermal distortion that's very significant, in addition to software adjusting for it, so
> that they can be more accurate, but I'm not sure what the mass budget would be or if it's
> reasonable. The reason I am rethinking it is we decided in the spin stabilized
> configuration to put the dry mass on different tethers than the thrusters to prevent
> optical interference, but if the dry mass must counter the weight of the thruster anyway
> we could reserve some mass (maybe 1kg on a 100kg thruster) that basically just keeps the
> temperature reasonably narrow in range.

### The premise checks out

Line 472 confirms it: "The tether length also separates the two packages by meters, which
lets one carry the thrusters and the other the optical sensors; the exhaust dissipates long
before reaching the far end." Line 397 gives the same reason for the propane plume, that it
should be aimed away from the electronics so it does not interfere with optical sensors.

So the architecture already commits to a sensing package on the far end of a tether from a
thruster package. And line 470 already says the two packages need not be identical, and that
one can be "a passive counterweight." The new thought is that the counterweight need not be
dead mass. If the sensing package has to balance the thruster package anyway, some of that
balance mass can be a thermal flywheel sitting under the optics.

That is a genuinely good structural argument. Spin balance turns mass that would otherwise
be a pure penalty into mass that is merely *reallocated*.

### The problem: the number is 4x the entire dry-mass budget

This is the thing to resolve first, and it is not small.

Line 459 sets the whole PuffSat dry mass at **250 g**, itemized as roughly 88 g propellant
tank, 48 g battery, 50 g valves and plumbing, three few-gram cameras, a gram-class gyro,
and 50 g of unsized remainder for structure, radio, and coast energy. That 250 g is split
across *both* tethered packages.

1 kg of PCM is four times that entire budget. Consequences to work through:

1. **The 1% claim.** Line 459 concludes that non-volatile solids are under 1% of total mass
   for a PuffSat carrying more than 25 kg of fuel. At 250 g that is comfortable. At 1.25 kg
   it needs rechecking against whichever PuffSat mass is being quoted. (Note the ambiguity in
   "1kg on a 100kg thruster" — the 100 kg figure matches the water bag of §`sec:icy_puffsat`,
   not a thruster. Worth confirming which denominator was meant.)
2. **Shrapnel and disposal.** The whole of §`sec:coordinator_node_dry_mass_disposal` exists
   because dry mass threatens the pusher plate. Five disposal strategies are listed, and
   their difficulty scales with the mass being disposed of. A 1 kg slug is a different
   proposition from a 125 g package.
3. **Spin balance.** Line 470 holds the mass-times-radius products equal across the pair.
   Adding 1 kg to the sensing side means either matching it on the thruster side, at 2 kg
   total, or shortening that arm substantially. Work out which is cheaper.
4. **Deorbit.** Line 533 sizes a ~4 m/s apogee burn per package for disposal from a 200 km
   perigee. That is velocity, so it is mass-independent, but the propellant to deliver it
   is not.

A cheaper version of the idea is worth sizing alongside the 1 kg case: the cameras are
few-gram parts, so a thermal mass that is *large relative to a camera* may be tens of grams
rather than a kilogram. Sizing question is how much energy has to be buffered over what
window, not how much mass is available.

### What the paper currently says about camera thermal distortion

Three places, and they need to stay consistent with whatever gets added:

- **Line 435** is the load-bearing treatment. A dichroic splits beacon and star light behind
  shared front optics, so the dominant distortion is common to both and cancels when
  differenced. "Thermal drift in the tracker optics is handled the same way. Heating bends
  the optical figure smoothly across the field, and a smooth shared distortion is what the
  star-differencing cancels." Gaia is the precedent for correcting a thermoelastic wobble a
  thousand times the accuracy target. Two hardware backups are already offered: a reflective
  narrowband front element that rejects out-of-band sunlight before it heats the optics, and
  edge-cooled synthetic diamond windows from gyrotron practice.
- **Line 385** says thermal distortion of the camera is explicitly *not* one of the Tier 3
  open sizing numbers.
- **Lines 1468–1470** retire it for the 4-radii periapsis leg on geometric grounds, since the
  optics ride in the shield's umbra where radiative load is nine orders below a sunlit
  aperture.

So PCM ballast would be a **third hardware backstop** alongside the reflective front element
and the diamond window, not a replacement for star-differencing. Seth's framing already says
this ("in addition to software adjusting for it"). Write it that way. Do not let it read as
though the software correction were insufficient, because line 385 currently banks on it.

Note also that most of the above is about the **target-side** tracker array, which does not
have a mass problem. The PuffSat-side cameras (lines 441–443) are the "optional redundancy
layer," and line 443 says their binding error is gyro drift over the terminal seconds, not
blur. Check whether thermal distortion is even the limiting term for the PuffSat cameras
before spending mass on it. If gyro drift dominates, a gram of better gyro beats a kilogram
of paraffin.

### Existing PCM precedent to reuse

Line 397 already puts a phase-change material on a PuffSat: "a phase change material like
paraffin wax on an aluminum mesh and running the propane over the wax," used for propane
thermal margin. It notes the wax is less mass-efficient than batteries but very cheap, and
that the mass stays small because total thrust is low. That paragraph is the model for how
to introduce PCM here, and it means no new PCM citation is needed for the concept itself.

### Suggested order of work

1. Decide the denominator (100 kg water bag? 25 kg PuffSat?) and recheck the 1% claim.
2. Size the actual thermal problem: absorbed watts on the sensing package over the coast and
   the terminal phase, allowable optics temperature swing, energy to buffer. Line 279 quotes
   10–15 W unshaded absorption for a reflective PuffSat, which is a starting number.
3. Only then pick a mass. The answer may be 30 g, not 1 kg.
4. If the mass lands large, write it into §`sec:spinning_tension_detail` at line 470 as a
   use for balance mass, and recheck §`sec:coordinator_node_dry_mass_disposal`.
5. Frame as a hardware backstop to star-differencing, consistent with line 435.
